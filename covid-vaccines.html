COVID-19 Vaccines: Testing Claims, Surveillance Failures & Evidence Gaps

A critical examination of what was tested, what was monitored, and what was never properly measured.

Sources: BMJ · The Lancet · NEJM · FDA · Health Canada · peer-reviewed literature Prepared · August 2026

Section 1Overview and purpose

This report examines three connected questions about the COVID-19 vaccines developed and authorised in 2020 and 2021: Were the vaccines as thoroughly tested as the public was told? What happened to the safety-surveillance systems designed to detect harm after authorisation? And was adequate evidence ever collected to answer whether widespread serious harm occurred?

The analysis draws exclusively on peer-reviewed medical literature, official regulatory documents, court records, and investigative reporting by major medical journals including the British Medical Journal (BMJ) and The Lancet. It does not rely on social-media claims, political statements, or unverified sources. The goal is to present the factual scientific and regulatory record as accurately as possible — acknowledging both what the evidence shows and where evidence was never collected.

Section 2 · Part OneWere the vaccines "thoroughly tested"?

This is not a simple yes-or-no question. The truthful answer has two parts: what was genuinely done, and where the process fell meaningfully short of the standard the public was often led to believe applied.

What was genuinely done

The COVID-19 vaccines underwent all three standard phases of clinical trials — Phase 1 (safety in small groups), Phase 2 (safety and preliminary efficacy in larger groups), and Phase 3 (large-scale efficacy and safety testing). The Phase 3 trials were genuinely large by historical standards:

The mRNA technology underpinning the Pfizer and Moderna vaccines had been in development for over a decade, and the speed of development was partly enabled by unprecedented government funding that allowed manufacturing to run in parallel with trials — not by skipping trial phases.

Where the process fell short of "thorough"

Emergency Use Authorisation (EUA) is not the same as full approval — but was frequently presented as equivalent. EUA requires a lower standard of evidence than standard full licensure: only two months of follow-up safety data for half the study population was required, compared with the years of follow-up normally expected for a new vaccine. Communications frequently did not clearly distinguish EUA from full approval. Pfizer received full FDA approval in August 2021 and Moderna in January 2022 — eight to fourteen months after initial EUA.

The "95% effectiveness" figure was relative risk reduction, not absolute. Pfizer's widely quoted 95% was a measure of relative risk reduction between trial groups. The absolute risk reduction — more practically meaningful for an individual — was approximately 0.84%, meaning roughly 119 people needed to be vaccinated under trial conditions to prevent one case of COVID-19. Both figures are scientifically valid but communicate very different impressions of benefit to a non-specialist audience.

A 95% relative risk reduction

Of the people who caught COVID-19 in the trial, far fewer were in the vaccinated group than the placebo group — about 95% fewer. This is the figure most people heard, and it is real.

The elderly and frail were barely represented in the trials. Diana Zuckerman, a consumer representative and FDA advisory-panel member, stated publicly that Pfizer's trial included only five people aged 75 and older who were diagnosed with COVID-19, making it "impossible to determine how effective the vaccine is for frail elderly patients." Subsequent peer-reviewed analysis confirmed that evidence for frail elderly subgroups "generalises poorly" from trial populations.

The trials were not designed to detect rare adverse events. Trials of 30,000–44,000 participants are powered to detect efficacy and common adverse events. An adverse event occurring in 1 in 50,000 or 1 in 100,000 doses would be statistically undetectable in a trial of this size. Detecting rare adverse events is precisely what post-market surveillance is supposed to do — the failure of which is addressed next.

Section 3 · Part TwoWhat happened to post-market safety surveillance?

Post-market surveillance — the ongoing monitoring of a medicine's safety after it reaches the general population — is supposed to be the safety net that catches what trials cannot. The COVID-19 vaccines were administered to billions of people, creating the largest pharmacovigilance challenge in history. What the peer-reviewed record shows is that the primary systems were inadequate to the task.

The VAERS system — a documented failure

The Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System (VAERS) is the United States' primary post-market vaccine-safety tool, jointly managed by the FDA and CDC. A formal investigation by the British Medical Journal found the following documented failures:

  • Reports were not followed up. Physicians who filed serious adverse-event reports — including a state medical examiner reporting a death — were never contacted by clinical reviewers, or were contacted many months later with no actionable outcome.
  • The public database was deliberately incomplete. The publicly accessible VAERS database contains only initial reports. Corrections, updates, case resolutions, and outcome data were maintained on a separate back-end system not accessible to the public, independent researchers, or the physicians who filed the original reports.
  • The system was understaffed and structurally overwhelmed. The volume of COVID-19 adverse-event reports was unprecedented — orders of magnitude above anything VAERS had previously managed — yet no proportionate expansion of review capacity was implemented in the critical first year.
  • The confidentiality justification was thin. Officials justified the incomplete public database as protecting patient confidentiality — a justification the BMJ characterised as inadequate given the public-health stakes.
VAERS, as operated during the roll-out, could neither reliably detect a safety signal nor confidently rule one out. Synthesis of the BMJ investigation

The control groups were dissolved — destroying long-term comparative data

This is arguably the most structurally consequential failure in the entire evidence base, and it was flagged at the time by senior scientists and the FDA itself:

  • The Pfizer and Moderna trial control groups — the tens of thousands of placebo recipients who made rigorous long-term comparison possible — were offered the active vaccine within approximately six months of authorisation.
  • Once vaccinated, these individuals were no longer a valid comparison group. The blinded, controlled comparison that is the foundation of rigorous medical evidence was permanently destroyed.
  • Multiple independent scientists described this at the time as a "disastrous" loss of scientifically irreplaceable data.
  • The FDA's own standard for full approval requires two years of follow-up data. When it granted full approval to Pfizer's vaccine in August 2021, that two-year data was not available — and, because the control group had been vaccinated, could never be collected.
  • Proposals from Stanford researchers and others for a blinded crossover design that would have preserved the long-term data were not adopted.

Subclinical harm was not being measured

Several categories of potential harm fell entirely outside what the surveillance systems were designed to detect:

Subclinical myocarditis. A peer-reviewed study in Thailand — published in a mainstream cardiology journal — measured cardiac troponin (a marker of heart-muscle stress) in vaccinated adolescents with no clinical symptoms, who would never appear in any adverse-event report. It found elevated troponin in a proportion of participants substantially exceeding the rate of clinically reported myocarditis. If confirmed more broadly, this suggests clinical myocarditis figures — already a confirmed signal — may significantly undercount actual cardiac impact.

Lipid-nanoparticle biodistribution. Pfizer's own pre-authorisation submission to Japan's regulator (obtained via freedom-of-information request) showed that the lipid nanoparticles delivering the mRNA did not remain at the injection site as had been assumed in public communications — distributing instead to the spleen, liver, bone marrow, and ovaries. This was available to regulators at authorisation but not communicated to the public or included in patient materials.

Menstrual irregularities. Reports of menstrual disruption were widespread from the earliest weeks of the roll-out. They were systematically dismissed by health authorities for several months before multiple peer-reviewed studies confirmed that COVID-19 vaccines can cause measurable menstrual-cycle changes. This pattern — initial dismissal followed by eventual confirmation — repeated across several categories of reported effects.

Long-term outcomes. By definition, a vaccine authorised in December 2020 with two months of safety follow-up cannot have peer-reviewed long-term safety data. No such data could exist at authorisation. This is not a criticism of the decision to authorise under emergency conditions; it is a statement of what was and was not known.

Section 4 · Part ThreeWas adequate evidence even collected?

This is the sharpest and most important question in the analysis. The statement that "no credible peer-reviewed evidence supports widespread serious harm" is technically accurate in a narrow sense — but logically incomplete, and potentially misleading, without the following context.

The fundamental logical problem

There is a fundamental distinction between two very different scientific statements:

  • "No evidence of harm" — meaning adequate systems were in place, evidence was properly collected, and that evidence did not reveal widespread harm.
  • "No collection of evidence capable of detecting harm" — meaning the surveillance infrastructure was structurally unable to detect, record, or confirm whether widespread harm occurred or not.

The peer-reviewed record — the BMJ investigation of VAERS, multiple analyses of trial design in The Lancet and the BMJ, and commentary from senior FDA, CDC, and academic researchers — supports the second description, not the first.

What independent peer-reviewed research has found

Where rigorous independent research was conducted — particularly in countries with strong health-registry systems — the findings were often more concerning than those from industry-funded or regulatory-led studies:

Nordic registry studies. Denmark, Sweden, Finland, and Norway — countries with comprehensive registries linking vaccination to hospital and death records — found higher rates of myocarditis than initially reported from U.S. surveillance, and raised questions about the benefit-risk balance of booster doses in younger, healthy males in particular.

The Cochrane review. The Cochrane Collaboration — recognised for the most rigorous systematic reviews in medicine — found that harms-data collection and reporting was inconsistent, incomplete, and not standardised across trials, and that definitive conclusions about the full harms profile could not be reached from trial data alone.

Industry-funding dominance. A systematic analysis found that the large majority of published COVID-19 vaccine-safety studies were funded by, or conducted by, researchers with direct or indirect pharmaceutical-industry ties. Truly independent post-market safety research was the exception rather than the rule.

The FDA data-transparency fight. The FDA initially proposed releasing Pfizer's complete clinical-trial data over 75 years — by 2097. A federal court ordered release within eight months. The full dataset was not available until March 2023 — more than two years after authorisation, and nearly two years into the worldwide programme.

What this means in plain language

How the evidence gap opened — a timeline

The gap was not a single decision but a chain of them. Select a point to read more.

  1. Jan 2020 — Genome published. China publishes the SARS-CoV-2 genome; PCR tests and mRNA vaccine design can begin within days.
  2. Mid 2020 — Phase 3 designed. Large trials are designed with placebo arms — but no plan to preserve those control groups for the long term.
  3. Dec 2020 — EUA granted. Authorisation comes with roughly two months of safety follow-up for half the participants.
  4. Early–mid 2021 — Control groups dissolved. Placebo recipients are vaccinated within ~6 months; the blinded comparison is permanently lost worldwide.
  5. 2021 — VAERS overwhelmed. An unprecedented surge of reports arrives with no proportionate expansion of review capacity.
  6. 2021–22 — Data withheld. The FDA proposes releasing Pfizer's data over 75 years; "safe and effective" messaging hardens.
  7. Mar 2023 — Window closed. A court forces release, but by then billions are vaccinated and the control groups are gone.

Section 5 · Part FourWhat the evidence does support

To ensure balance, the following is equally supported by the peer-reviewed record and should be clearly stated:

  • The vaccines produced a genuine, measurable immune response in the vast majority of recipients and substantially reduced serious illness and death from COVID-19 in the period immediately following vaccination — particularly in older and more vulnerable populations.
  • A modelling study in The Lancet estimated that COVID-19 vaccines prevented between 14 and 20 million deaths globally in their first year of deployment.
  • COVID-19 itself causes serious cardiovascular harm — including myocarditis — at rates generally higher than those associated with vaccination in the general adult population. In the context of active viral circulation, the vaccinated-versus- unvaccinated comparison favoured vaccination for most adults.
  • The confirmed post-market signals — primarily myocarditis in young males after mRNA vaccination — are real, but were described as predominantly mild and self-resolving in the majority of documented clinical cases, though rare severe cases were reported.
  • No peer-reviewed study has established a causal mechanism for a large category of undisclosed serious harm that regulators and manufacturers deliberately concealed. The documented failures here are primarily failures of surveillance design and data transparency — not proof of coordinated concealment of known widespread harm.

Section 6Conclusion

The intellectually honest conclusion — supported by the published peer-reviewed record, and not by social media, political commentary, or unverified claims — is the following:

The COVID-19 vaccines underwent real clinical trials and produced real benefits, particularly in older and more vulnerable populations during a genuine global health emergency. At the same time, the testing process was materially shorter than standard vaccine approval; the public was not always clearly informed of the distinction between emergency and full authorisation; the post-market surveillance systems were demonstrably inadequate; the trial control groups were dissolved before long-term safety data could be collected; the most vulnerable elderly populations were barely represented in the trials; and the primary data-transparency mechanism required a court order to function.

The consequence is not that the vaccines were certainly harmful in ways that were concealed. The consequence is that we cannot say with scientific confidence that they were not — because the evidence infrastructure required to answer that question was never properly established or maintained.

For the scientific community, this represents an important and unresolved gap in the evidence base. For regulatory bodies, it represents structural lessons that should inform how emergency authorisation, post-market surveillance, and data transparency are handled in future public-health emergencies. For individual patients — particularly older adults with existing health conditions — it underscores the importance of personalised medical advice from a physician who knows their specific circumstances.

Section 7About the sources

This report draws on: peer-reviewed articles and editorial investigations in the British Medical Journal (BMJ), The Lancet, New England Journal of Medicine, European Heart Journal, and Vaccine; official regulatory documents from the U.S. FDA, the European Medicines Agency (EMA), and Health Canada; Cochrane Collaboration systematic reviews; freedom-of-information releases from Pfizer's regulatory submissions; court records from the FOIA litigation requiring release of Pfizer's clinical-trial data; and on-the-record statements by named officials and researchers in their professional capacities. Social-media content, anonymous claims, and unverified reports were not used as sources.

A reminder

This analysis is a critique of evidence quality — not a claim that the vaccines caused concealed harm, and not a recommendation for or against vaccination. Individuals, especially older adults, should rely on personalised medical advice.
