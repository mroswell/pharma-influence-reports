These exchanges expand on the COVID-19 vaccine report. They are grouped by theme and lightly edited for length; the substance follows the same evidence-based, deliberately hedged approach as the report itself. Click any question to expand.

Reminder

Informational only, not medical advice. These answers concern the quality of the evidence and the incentives around it — not a claim that harm occurred, and not a recommendation for or against vaccination.

I · The worldwide evidence gap

To what do you attribute the lack of COVID-vaccine follow-up studies worldwide?

It is not a single cause but the result of six converging structural factors:

  • Loss of control groups. Once Pfizer and Moderna vaccinated their placebo groups, no long-term randomised comparison could ever be built. Every country inherited this limitation — a global structural failure, not a national one.
  • Weak surveillance systems. Most countries rely on passive reporting like VAERS, which under-counts events, cannot verify causation, and cannot detect subclinical or long-term outcomes.
  • Funding and career incentives. Most safety research was industry- or government-funded with a pro-vaccine policy stance; publishing findings that contradicted the dominant message carried professional risk.
  • Limited registry capacity. Only a handful of countries (the Nordic states) had the linked vaccination–hospitalisation–mortality registries needed for strong follow-up — and even they could not study what the missing control groups removed.
  • A chilling effect. Messaging framed vaccines as the exit strategy and questioning as "misinformation," producing self-censorship rather than outright censorship.
  • Emergency authorisation. EUA compressed timelines and eliminated long-term planning; once billions were vaccinated, the window for controlled studies closed.

None of these imply that widespread harm occurred — but together they mean we cannot scientifically confirm that it did not.

If those factors were identifiable, why weren't they investigated? And was the threat exaggerated?

The rush explains the initial shortcuts; it does not explain the multi-year absence of follow-up. That requires a deeper explanation — institutional, not conspiratorial:

  • Once governments and agencies declared the vaccines "safe and effective" and "the science settled," any study capable of contradicting those claims became politically dangerous. Institutions rarely investigate failures that could expose their own mistakes.
  • The data needed for follow-up no longer existed once the control groups were dissolved — a design decision, not a consequence of speed.
  • On the threat: COVID was genuinely more dangerous to the elderly and frail than seasonal flu, but public messaging communicated it as a uniform threat rather than a steeply age-stratified one. Framing a virus as an existential, uniform danger prioritises speed over rigor and messaging over measurement.

The honest synthesis: follow-up was not conducted because institutions had already committed to a narrative that follow-up might undermine — a claim about institutional behaviour under pressure, not about criminal intent.

What actually motivated the lack of follow-up?

Institutional incentives, not scientific ones. Follow-up could reveal problems, and institutions had no incentive to look for problems after publicly committing to a position. Contributing motives included:

  • Political self-protection after "safe and effective / settled science" declarations;
  • Bureaucratic inertia — agencies were rewarded for rollout, compliance, and booster uptake, not for safety rigor or admitting uncertainty;
  • Academic career risk in studying vaccine harms;
  • Industry incentives favouring speed over long-term safety studies;
  • Fear that acknowledging uncertainty would reduce public trust and increase hesitancy.

In an emergency, follow-up should be more rigorous than usual. Instead it was almost non-existent.

Does this not expose some possible corruption?

It exposes structural incentives that can look like corruption but are better understood as institutional behaviour under pressure. When institutions suppress uncertainty, avoid collecting data, dissolve control groups, and withhold trial information, the outcome resembles corruption because it produces the same result — a lack of accountability.

But the underlying motivations were mostly political, bureaucratic, reputational, and economic rather than overtly criminal. No one needed to be bribed; no conspiracy was required. That distinction matters: this is not corruption in the legal sense of bribery, but it can fairly be described as a corruption of the scientific process.

II · COVID in context

COVID was said to be more dangerous to the elderly and infirm — how is that different from any other flu? And how did scientists know so much so fast?

COVID vs. seasonal flu. All respiratory viruses hit the elderly hardest, but COVID was worse on several measures: an infection-fatality rate for seniors of roughly 5–15% in 2020–21 versus about 0.1–0.5% for flu (an order of magnitude higher); far higher rates of severe pneumonia, ARDS, microvascular clotting, and multi-organ involvement; and faster, more silent spread. So COVID was more dangerous to the elderly than flu — though the public messaging did not clearly convey that the risk was steeply age-stratified.

How scientists knew so much so quickly. The SARS-CoV-2 genome was published on 10 January 2020; with the sequence, PCR tests and vaccine design follow quickly. mRNA platforms and lipid-nanoparticle delivery already existed; prior SARS-1 and MERS research had mapped the spike protein and ACE2 binding; and governments funded design, testing, and manufacturing in parallel rather than in sequence. That is why the timeline looked fast.

Does the speed imply a lab origin? No. A fast vaccine does not require prior knowledge of the virus — once the genome is public, mRNA design takes days; the long part is testing, not designing. The origin question (natural spillover vs. lab-associated accident) remains debated, but vaccine speed is evidence for neither, and the genome does not show the signatures typical of deliberate engineering.

Is this lack of thorough efficacy and safety testing typical of new vaccines or medicines?

No. Normal vaccine development runs 5–10 years: years of preclinical work, Phase 1–2 over months to years, Phase 3 often several years with tens of thousands of participants, regulatory review, and indefinite post-market surveillance.

COVID vaccines were authorised under EUA in under a year, with Phase 3 trials running roughly 3–4 months before authorisation and about two months of safety follow-up for half the participants. Dissolving control groups is never done in normal development. The compressed timeline and overwhelmed surveillance were exceptions created by emergency conditions — not the norm for how vaccines or medicines are typically tested.

If rushing the solution was understandable, wouldn't even more comprehensive follow-up be necessary?

Yes — that is the scientifically correct position, and it is a foundational principle of pharmacovigilance. When a product is developed quickly, authorised on limited data, deployed to billions using a novel platform, and given to populations barely represented in trials, post-market surveillance becomes the primary safety system, not a secondary one.

Instead, the opposite happened: the rush justified shortcuts; the shortcuts (especially dissolving control groups) eliminated the ability to do follow-up; and the lack of follow-up was then justified by the rush. It is a circular failure.

III · Canada vs. the United States

Was the United States meaningfully better than Canada at studying vaccine efficacy and side effects?

No. The U.S. was bigger, faster, and more complex — but not better. It had more surveillance systems (VAERS, VSD, BEST, CMS data, hospital networks), yet the primary one, VAERS, was found structurally incapable of reliably detecting or ruling out a signal. It produced many effectiveness studies — which measure how well vaccines prevent hospitalisation — but very few safety studies, because the control groups were gone, data access was restricted, and institutions had little incentive to investigate harms.

Crucially, the U.S. dissolved its placebo groups first and withheld Pfizer's trial data (initially proposing a 75-year release; a court forced release, with the full dataset available only in March 2023). Canada relied on U.S. trial data, so Canada's evidence gap is partly a U.S. transparency gap. Both failed — in different ways — and the outcome was identical evidence gaps. As the report puts it: the U.S. created the global evidence gap; Canada inherited it.
