Pharma Contributions and Senate Drug-Pricing Votes

Section 1Overview

This report examines the relationship between pharmaceutical-industry campaign contributions to United States senators and those senators' voting records on major drug-pricing legislation between 2022 and 2024. The data draws on Federal Election Commission filings compiled by OpenSecrets.org, and on official Senate roll-call records.

A 2023 analysis found that members of Congress who voted against letting Medicare negotiate prescription-drug prices received, on average, more than three times the pharmaceutical-industry donations of those who voted in favour.

This report presents the key votes, the top recipients of pharma money among currently serving senators, and a side-by-side comparison of contributions and voting behaviour.

Reading this chart honestly

Among these currently serving senators, the vote split was strictly along party lines, and the two groups' average contributions are close — roughly $162k for the "against" group versus $149k for the "for" group. In other words, within this small subset the money does not cleanly predict the vote beyond party. The widely cited "more than three times" figure comes from a broader 2023 analysis across the whole of Congress — not from these fifteen senators. The chart shows the real pattern: pharma money flows to senators of both parties, and the IRA vote itself was party-line.

Section 4The party-line pattern — the numbers

Roll-call reality

All 50 Republican senators voted against the Inflation Reduction Act drug-pricing provisions in August 2022. All 50 Democratic and Independent senators who supported the bill voted in favour. Final passage: 51–50 — only because Vice-President Kamala Harris cast the deciding vote as President of the Senate. In September 2024, Senate Republicans again voted as a bloc — 49 to 51 — to block expansion of Medicare drug-price negotiation.

Key finding from campaign-finance analysis

Senators who voted against Medicare drug-price negotiation received, on average, more than three times the pharmaceutical-industry donations compared with senators who voted in favour — according to a 2023 review of FEC records.

Section 5Notable individual cases

Bill Cassidy (R-Louisiana) — $290,375

The largest pharma-funded currently serving senator. Cassidy sits on the Senate Health, Education, Labor and Pensions (HELP) Committee and voted against the IRA drug-pricing provisions. He has publicly cited concerns about pharmaceutical innovation as a reason to oppose government price negotiation.

Marsha Blackburn (R-Tennessee) — $316,656

The highest pharma total among currently serving senators. She voted against all IRA drug-pricing provisions and against the $35 insulin-cap amendment, arguing that Medicare price negotiation constitutes government price-setting.

John Barrasso (R-Wyoming) — $204,761

As Senate Republican Whip, Barrasso helped coordinate the party-line opposition to the IRA and has been a vocal critic of Medicare drug-price negotiation.

Jon Tester (D-Montana) — $401,885

Second-highest overall in the cycle. Tester was one of three Democrats who originally blocked the broader Build Back Better Act in 2021, which contained more expansive drug-pricing reforms. He ultimately voted for the scaled-down IRA in 2022, and lost his 2024 re-election bid.

Kyrsten Sinema (then D-Arizona) — $17,600

Though not among the top pharma recipients in the 2023–24 cycle, Sinema was a key holdout who insisted on scaling back the drug-pricing provisions in Build Back Better before they were reduced for inclusion in the IRA. She did not seek re-election in 2024.

Section 6Important caveats

Correlation is not causation. Campaign-finance data shows patterns of giving and voting but does not prove that contributions caused any senator's vote. Senators may hold genuine ideological positions, respond to constituents, or reflect their state's economy (e.g., large biotech or pharmaceutical industries).

Pharma gives to both parties. The industry deliberately spreads contributions across Democratic and Republican senators, particularly those on health-relevant committees (Finance, HELP, Energy and Commerce) regardless of party.

Committee assignment matters. Senators on the Finance and HELP committees tend to receive higher pharma contributions because they have direct jurisdiction over Medicare policy and drug-pricing regulation — which can create the appearance of influence even where none exists.

Votes are complex. Many senators who voted against the IRA cited concerns unrelated to drug pricing — the bill's tax provisions, climate spending, or general budget concerns.

Single-cycle snapshot. These figures cover the 2023–2024 cycle only. Career totals — which can run into the millions for senior senators — often tell a more complete story of long-term industry relationships.

Section 7Total industry spending in context

The pharmaceutical and health-products industry spent more than $370 million on federal lobbying in a single recent year — making it consistently one of the most aggressive special interests in Washington. In the 2023–2024 cycle alone, pharma and health-product PACs disbursed roughly $16 million directly to federal candidates; adding employee and affiliated donations brings the total to about $86.5 million.

By comparison, the IRA's Medicare drug-price-negotiation program is projected to save the federal government about $287 billion over ten years — and to directly reduce out-of-pocket costs for millions of American seniors.

About the data — Contribution figures: FEC filings compiled by OpenSecrets. Voting records: official U.S. Senate roll-call archives. Lobbying figures: OpenSecrets' lobbying database. Figures cover the 2023–2024 cycle and include PAC contributions plus donations of $200+ from pharma-affiliated individuals.
